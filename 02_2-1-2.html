2.1.2 Heating Values and Energy Release

ME 3115 • CO2 • Lesson 2.1 • LO 2.1

Learning focus: Distinguish HHV from LHV, apply the first law to a reacting steady-flow system, and connect fuel energy to the heat input \(Q_{in}\) used in Unit I.

Lecture notes

The enthalpy of combustion is the difference between the enthalpy of the reactants and the enthalpy of the products when both are at the same reference temperature (25 °C). Per unit mass of fuel it is the heating value.

Heating value (heat released at constant reference temperature)

\[Q_{\mathrm{HV}}=\frac{H_R-H_P}{m_f}\quad\left(T_R=T_P=25^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\right)\]

Source: Heywood, Internal Combustion Engine Fundamentals (2nd ed.), Ch. 3 (heats of reaction); Ferguson & Kirkpatrick, Internal Combustion Engines: Applied Thermosciences, Ch. 3; Pulkrabek, Engineering Fundamentals of the Internal Combustion Engine, Ch. 4.

The higher heating value (HHV) assumes the water in the products is condensed to liquid. The lower heating value (LHV) assumes it stays as vapor. Engines exhaust hot vapor, so engine efficiencies use LHV. For a fuel with hydrogen mass fraction \(H\), each kg of fuel forms \(9H\) kg of water.

Lower heating value from higher heating value

\[\mathrm{LHV}=\mathrm{HHV}-9\,\mathrm{H}\,h_{fg}\]

\[h_{fg}\approx2442\ \mathrm{kJ/kg}\]

\(\mathrm{H}\) is the hydrogen mass fraction of the fuel.

Source: Heywood, Internal Combustion Engine Fundamentals (2nd ed.), Ch. 3; Faires, Thermodynamics (combustion and gas-mixture chapters) (higher and lower heating value); Pulkrabek, Engineering Fundamentals of the Internal Combustion Engine, Ch. 4.

FuelTypical LHV (MJ/kg)Remark
Gasoline44SI engines
Diesel42.5CI engines
Natural gas48 – 50mostly CH₄
Hydrogen120highest per kg, low per m³

Worked Example 2 — HHV to LHV

A fuel has HHV = 47,900 kJ/kg and hydrogen mass fraction 0.14. Find its LHV.

\[m_{\mathrm{H_2O}}=9\,\mathrm{H}=9(0.14)=1.26\ \mathrm{kg/kg\ fuel}\]

\[\mathrm{LHV}=47900-1.26\,(2442)\]

\[\mathrm{LHV}=47900-3077=44823\ \mathrm{kJ/kg}\]

Answer: LHV = 44,823 kJ/kg.

Method from: Faires, Thermodynamics (combustion and gas-mixture chapters); Heywood, Internal Combustion Engine Fundamentals (2nd ed.), Ch. 3.

First law for a combustor

Steady-flow first law for a reacting system, and adiabatic flame temperature

\[Q-W=H_P-H_R\]

\[H_P\left(T_{ad}\right)=H_R\]

\(H_R\) and \(H_P\) are the enthalpy flows of reactants and products. For no heat loss and no work, \(T_{ad}\) is the product temperature at which \(H_P=H_R\).

Source: Heywood, Internal Combustion Engine Fundamentals (2nd ed.), Ch. 3 (first law for reacting systems, adiabatic flame temperature); Faires, Thermodynamics (combustion and gas-mixture chapters).

For hydrocarbons burning in air near \(\phi=1\), \(T_{ad}\) is about 2200–2300 K. It falls with excess air because the same energy heats more nitrogen, and dissociation at high temperature lowers it below the ideal value.

From fuel energy to the heat input of the cycle

Unit I treated \(Q_{in}\) as given. In a real engine, \(Q_{in}\) is the fuel energy that actually burns, and brake thermal efficiency is referred to the LHV.

Heat input and brake thermal efficiency

\[Q_{\mathrm{in}}=\eta_c\,m_f\,\mathrm{LHV}\]

\[\eta_b=\frac{\dot{W}_b}{\dot{m}_f\,q_c}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{bsfc}\cdot q_c},\quad q_c=\mathrm{LHV}\]

\(\eta_c\) is combustion efficiency (page 2.5.3), \(m_f\) is the fuel mass per cycle, and \(q_c\) is the LHV (Unit I Formula Reference notation).

Source: Unit I Formula Reference page; Heywood, Ch. 2 and Ch. 3; Pulkrabek, Ch. 2.

Note!

Page 1.3.1 defined \(\eta_{th}=W_{net}/Q_{in}\) for the ideal cycle. The brake thermal efficiency is the same idea with \(Q_{in}=\dot m_f\,\mathrm{LHV}\) and brake work. The Otto efficiency \(\eta_{Otto}=1-1/r^{k-1}\) is the ceiling; fuel energy that does not burn, and heat that goes to coolant and exhaust, is why \(\eta_b\) is lower.
