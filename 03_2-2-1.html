2.2.1 Fuel Families and Quality Numbers

ME 3115 • CO2 • Lesson 2.2 • LO 2.1

Learning focus: Compare solid, liquid and gaseous fuels by how each is specified, and explain octane number, cetane number and volatility.

Lecture notes

TypeExamplesSpecified byTypical use
Solidcoal, coke, biomass, charcoalproximate and ultimate analysis (mass basis)boilers, gasifiers
Liquidgasoline, diesel, kerosene, ethanol, biodieselspecific gravity or API, distillation curve, vapor pressure, octane or cetane, viscosity, flash pointSI and CI engines
Gaseousnatural gas, LPG, biogas, producer gas, hydrogenvolumetric (mole) analysis, heating value per m³, Wobbe indexgas engines, burners, turbines

Proximate analysis of a solid fuel reports moisture, volatile matter, fixed carbon and ash. Ultimate analysis reports the mass fractions of C, H, O, N, S, ash and moisture, which is what the combustion calculations need.

Quality numbers for engines

  • Octane number measures resistance to knock (iso-octane = 100, n-heptane = 0). It limits the compression ratio of an SI engine.
  • Cetane number measures ignition quality. A high cetane number means a short ignition delay and smoother diesel combustion.
  • Volatility (distillation curve, Reid vapor pressure) controls cold starting and vapor lock.
  • Flash point, viscosity and sulfur affect safety, injection behavior and emissions.

Gaseous fuels mix well with air and burn cleanly, but they have lower energy per unit volume, so in a port-fueled engine the gas displaces air and reduces volumetric efficiency \(e_v\).

Note!

Page 1.2.2 showed that SI engines are limited to compression ratios of 8:1 to 12:1 by knock, while CI engines run 14:1 to 22:1. The octane and cetane numbers introduced here are the fuel properties behind those limits. Through \(\eta_{Otto}=1-1/r^{k-1}\) the fuel quality therefore sets the efficiency ceiling.
