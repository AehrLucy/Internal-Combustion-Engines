2.6.2 Boiler Heat and Mass Balance

ME 3115 • CO2 • Lesson 2.6 • LO 2.2

Learning focus: Compute boiler efficiency and the dry flue gas loss, and relate both to excess air.

Lecture notes

A boiler turns fuel energy into steam enthalpy. Its losses are: dry flue gas, moisture in the fuel and from burning hydrogen, unburned carbon in the ash, CO in the flue gas, and radiation. Dry flue gas is usually the largest.

Boiler efficiency (direct method)

\[\eta_{\mathrm{boiler}}=\frac{\dot{m}_s\left(h_s-h_{fw}\right)}{\dot{m}_f\,\mathrm{LHV}}\]

\(\dot m_s\) steam flow, \(h_s\) steam enthalpy, \(h_{fw}\) feedwater enthalpy.

Source: Faires, Thermodynamics (combustion and gas-mixture chapters) (steam generator analysis); Maleev, Internal Combustion Engines: Theory and Design (fuels and combustion chapters) (boiler heat balance); MIT OpenCourseWare 2.61 (Internal Combustion Engines) and 16.050 (Thermal Energy).

Dry flue gas loss

\[\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{dfg}}=\dot{m}_{\mathrm{dfg}}\,c_p\left(T_{\mathrm{stack}}-T_0\right)\]

More excess air raises \(\dot m_{dfg}\), so the stack carries away more heat.

Source: Faires, Thermodynamics (combustion and gas-mixture chapters); Maleev, Internal Combustion Engines: Theory and Design (fuels and combustion chapters).

Worked Example 10 — Boiler efficiency

The coal of Example 3 (LHV 28,524 kJ/kg) is fired at 1.0 kg/s and makes 9.0 kg/s of steam with \(h_s-h_{fw}=2800\) kJ/kg.

\[\dot{Q}_{u}=9.0\,(2800)=25200\ \mathrm{kW}\]

\[\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{in}}=1.0\,(28524)=28524\ \mathrm{kW}\]

\[\eta_{\mathrm{boiler}}=\frac{25200}{28524}=0.883\]

Answer: \(\eta_{boiler}=88.3\%\).

The remaining 3,324 kW is split among flue gas, unburned carbon, ash and radiation.

Method from: Faires, Thermodynamics (combustion and gas-mixture chapters); Maleev, Internal Combustion Engines: Theory and Design (fuels and combustion chapters).

Practical rule: use the stack O₂ reading to set the excess air, and the stack temperature to judge heat recovery. Too little air produces CO and unburned carbon. The best setting keeps excess air just above the point where CO appears.

Note:

The method is the same first-law bookkeeping used for the engine, and for the Brayton and Dual cycles of Unit I: energy in, useful output, and the losses that make up the difference.
