2.1.1 Combustion Reactions and Stoichiometric Air

ME 3115 • CO2 • Lesson 2.1; LO 2.1

Learning focus: Write and balance the combustion reaction of a hydrocarbon fuel in air and compute the stoichiometric air-fuel ratio.

Lecture notes

A fuel is a substance whose chemical bonds release energy when rearranged into more stable products. In an engine the oxidizer is air. Treat dry air as 21% O₂ and 79% N₂ by volume, so every kmol of O₂ arrives with 3.76 kmol of N₂. The molar mass of air is 28.97 kg/kmol.

In complete combustion with exactly enough oxygen (stoichiometric combustion), all carbon becomes CO₂ and all hydrogen becomes H₂O. Nitrogen is carried through as an inert diluent. Balance the elements in this order: carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen.

Stoichiometric combustion of a hydrocarbon \(\mathrm{C}_x\mathrm{H}_y\) in air

\[\mathrm{C}_x\mathrm{H}_y+\left(x+\frac{y}{4}\right)\left(\mathrm{O}_2+3.76\,\mathrm{N}_2\right)\]

\[\rightarrow\;x\,\mathrm{CO}_2+\frac{y}{2}\,\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}+3.76\left(x+\frac{y}{4}\right)\mathrm{N}_2\]

Source: Heywood, Internal Combustion Engine Fundamentals (2nd ed.), Ch. 3 (combustion stoichiometry); Pulkrabek, Engineering Fundamentals of the Internal Combustion Engine, Ch. 4; Faires, Thermodynamics (combustion and gas-mixture chapters).

Stoichiometric air-fuel ratio

\[\left(\frac{A}{F}\right)_s=\frac{4.76\left(x+y/4\right)M_{\mathrm{air}}}{12.011\,x+1.008\,y}\]

where \(M_{air}=28.97\) kg/kmol, and 12.011 and 1.008 are the atomic masses of C and H.

Source: Heywood, Internal Combustion Engine Fundamentals (2nd ed.), Ch. 3; Pulkrabek, Engineering Fundamentals of the Internal Combustion Engine, Ch. 4.

Commercial gasoline is a blend, not pure octane, so its stoichiometric ratio is usually quoted as about 14.7. Diesel is about 14.5, and methane is 17.2.

Worked Example 1 — Stoichiometric A/F of octane

Find the stoichiometric air-fuel ratio of C₈H₁₈.

\[\mathrm{C_8H_{18}}+12.5\left(\mathrm{O_2}+3.76\,\mathrm{N_2}\right)\rightarrow8\,\mathrm{CO_2}+9\,\mathrm{H_2O}+47\,\mathrm{N_2}\]

\[m_{\mathrm{air}}=12.5\times4.76\times28.97=1723.7\ \mathrm{kg}\]

\[m_f=8(12.011)+18(1.008)=114.23\ \mathrm{kg}\]

\[\left(\frac{A}{F}\right)_s=\frac{1723.7}{114.23}=15.09\]

Answer: \((A/F)_s=15.09\) kg air per kg fuel.

Common error: using 12.5 instead of 12.5 × 4.76 kmol of air.

Method from: Heywood, Internal Combustion Engine Fundamentals (2nd ed.), Ch. 3; Pulkrabek, Engineering Fundamentals of the Internal Combustion Engine, Ch. 4.

Note!

In the air-standard Otto cycle (page 1.2.3) heat addition replaces combustion. Here we begin to ask what mass of fuel and air actually supplies that heat. Real cycles also differ from air-standard ones because of the changed composition of the products (page 1.1.1, assumptions of air-standard analysis).

Class Activity — Hand-calculation workshop

In pairs, write and balance the reaction for propane (C₃H₈) and methane, then compute \((A/F)_s\) for each. Compare with octane and explain why the hydrogen-rich fuels need more air per kg of fuel.

Check Your Understanding

  1. Why does 1 kmol of O₂ bring 3.76 kmol of N₂?
  2. Write the stoichiometric reaction of ethanol, C₂H₅OH, in air. (Its oxygen content changes the O₂ demand.)
  3. What happens to \((A/F)_s\) as the hydrogen fraction of a fuel increases?
